Training plan — 14-month rescue border collie mix, South Bay

Two problems, and they are different problems. Pulling is a mechanical skill the dog hasn't learned. Barking at every dog is an emotional response to arousal and frustration. They need separate training and they should not be worked in the same session until you've made progress on each alone.

1. Hire, or do it yourself?

Do it yourself — but buy about $300 of structure. Don't buy a $2,400 private package as your first move.

Why DIY is the right default here

  • The problem is common and not dangerous. Pulling plus barking/lunging, adolescent rescue, no bite history. This is the single most common complaint reward-based trainers see, and the protocols are documented and public.
  • You are the one holding the leash. Reactivity work is won by repetition at the right distance over weeks. Nobody can do those reps for you. Notably, the local private trainers frame it this way themselves — Stacy's Wag'N'Train describes private lessons as teaching you to train your dog, as opposed to her separate "I train" day-training programme.
  • 14 months is a good age to catch it. Peak adolescent arousal. A lot of this softens with maturity plus consistent handling plus an adequate mental outlet — which, for a collie, is the real lever.
  • Cost. Private packages here run $845–$2,400 for six weeks. The gear kit is $60–$120, once.

Why not pure DIY

The one thing that's genuinely hard to self-teach is threshold judgement — knowing how far away is far enough. Get it wrong and every session is a rehearsal of the reaction, and the dog gets worse. Marker timing is a mechanical skill most owners get late, you can't see your own leash tension, and real walks won't give you controlled exposures. A structured class supplies all three cheaply.

Escalate to a professional immediately if

  • Any bite or contact with a dog or person
  • Redirected aggression — dog turns on the leash, your hands, your clothing
  • No measurable change after 4–6 weeks of consistent work
  • Reactivity spreading to people, bikes, cars, or appearing through windows at home
  • You're walking the dog less because you dread it

One non-negotiable on method

Reward-based only. The AVSAB position statement recommends that only reward-based training methods be used for all aspects of dog training and behavior modification, and that aversive methods — including electronic collars, prong collars, choke chains and leash corrections — should not be used under any circumstances. There are no exceptions to this standard, even for dogs with aggressive behaviour. Practically: punishing the bark suppresses the display and leaves the arousal underneath. Decline any trainer marketing themselves as "balanced" or offering e-collar work.

3. The four-week plan

Two rules run through all of it:

  1. Distance is the dial. Every problem is solved by adding distance first.
  2. Don't train pulling and reactivity in the same session until Week 3.

Week 1 — Stop the backsliding

Goal: zero rehearsed reactions. No training yet. Every bark-and-lunge is a rep that strengthens the behaviour.

Gear ($60–$120): front-clip harness — a body harness reduces pressure on the neck and gives you more control, with the Freedom No-Pull's two contact points, or the Balance Harness or Easy Walk as alternatives; a regular 6 ft leash about 1 inch wide — no bungee, no retractable; a 15–30 ft long line; a waist treat pouch; and high-value treats that are soft and smelly — turkey hot dog, string cheese, chicken breast. Kibble will not compete with another dog.

Management, all week: walk at low-traffic times. Pick routes with escape options — wide streets, cul-de-sacs, empty lots. Avoid narrow sidewalks and the dog park perimeter entirely. Practise fast U-turns as a way of luring the dog away from a distraction — ten reps on a quiet street with no dog present, so it's automatic when you need it. If a dog appears too close: turn, cross, or step behind a parked car and feed until it's gone. That's the plan, not cheating.

Baseline measurement (once, 15 min): find the distance at which your dog notices another dog but can still take a treat and look back at you. Usually at least 20 feet, though some dogs need considerably more — a leash-reactive border collie might start triggering at anything closer than 50 feet, meaning you'd begin work at 60. Write the number down. It's your progress metric for four weeks.

Attention foundation (5 min/day indoors): charge a marker word ("yes" → treat, 20x), name response (10 reps), hand target (10 reps).

Advance when: you know your threshold in feet, the dog turns to its name indoors, and you got through the week with fewer than two full reactions.

Week 2 — Leash mechanics + Engage/Disengage

Track A — loose leash (10 min, 5×/wk, driveway or empty lot). The rule doing most of the work: tight leash means forward motion stops, every time, including when you're in a hurry. Become a tree; move again only when the leash goes slack. Feed at your seam, not out in front — where you feed is where the dog will want to be. Count steps of slack leash and reset to one on any pull. Change direction every 15–20 seconds so the dog tracks you instead of towing you.

Split your walks: a short strict "training walk" and a long-line "sniff walk" with different rules. Enforcing loose-leash on every outing for a collie will burn you both out.

Track B — Engage/Disengage (10–15 min, 3×/wk), at or beyond your threshold distance. You sit and watch dogs; you don't walk past them. The critical constraint is that you only play this when the dog is under threshold.

  • Stage 1: dog looks at the other dog → mark the instant it looks → feed. You're paying for noticing calmly.
  • Stage 2: once that's fluent, wait a beat and mark only when the dog looks and then turns back to you on its own. This is the sequence — rewarding glances at dogs, then voluntary disengagement.

Work in roughly five-minute increments, then take a break and let the dog relax. Even sub-threshold exposure accumulates stress, so keep sessions brief and positive.

Too close looks like: can't take the treat, hard stare, stiff body, won't break the look. Add 10–15 feet immediately. When unsure, always add distance.

Advance when: 10–15 slack-leash steps in a quiet place, and the dog comes back to you after looking at a dog at least half the time.

Week 3 — Close the distance, add movement

Only advance if Week 2's criteria were actually met. This is not a schedule you owe anyone.

  • Move 5–10 feet closer. One variable at a time.
  • Add movement: walk parallel to the trigger rather than sitting still. Head-on approaches are the hardest thing you'll ever ask for and are months away, not this month.
  • Pattern game "1-2-3" — say the count, feed on three, walking as you go. Gives the dog a predictable rhythm and gives you something to do with your voice other than tense up.
  • Move the leash work to a new but still dog-free location. Expect performance to halve. That's context specificity, not disobedience — drop criteria, pay more, rebuild.
  • Reward every unprompted check-in heavily. That becomes your default walking behaviour.
  • Once or twice, run a real 15-minute walk with both rule sets on. Bail early if it goes badly; ending on a bad rep is worse than a short walk.

Collie-specific, don't skip: 15 min/day of brain work outside the reactivity training — scent games, flirt pole, trick training, shaping, snuffle mat. An under-stimulated collie has a higher baseline arousal, and everything else you're doing is fighting that number.

Week 4 — Real walks and an honest reassessment

  • Three real walks on your normal routes with the full toolkit.
  • One deliberate closer pass per walk, with a bail-out ready. One.
  • Thin the treat rate on loose-leash walking to intermittent. Keep reactivity payments at full rate — that's emotional work, not obedience.
  • Re-measure the threshold distance exactly as in Week 1. Compare.

What normal progress actually looks like: not a cured dog. Shorter threshold distance, much faster recovery after a reaction (seconds, not the rest of the walk), less intense reactions, and you spotting triggers before the dog does. Reactivity is a months-to-a-year project. Four weeks buys you a manageable walk.

3. The first four weeks

Two protocols running in parallel: loose-leash mechanics (daily, low stakes) and counter-conditioning to dogs (3–4 short sessions a week, always under threshold). Every week is broken out as its own subtask with the full detail.

Rules that start on day one and never stop

  • Never let the leash go tight while he's looking at a dog. The leash stops a fearful dog fleeing, so it switches to "fight" instead, and for a frustrated dog it blocks what he wants and adds to the frustration; your own stress also travels down a taut leash, so keep it loose and breathe slowly.
  • Cross the street or U-turn early, every time. No head-on passes on narrow pavements.
  • Walk at off-peak hours for the next month. Fewer dogs = fewer rehearsals.
  • Watch for trigger stacking. Lots of small triggers accumulate — each one alone wouldn't tip him over, but piled up they do. A bad session usually means he was already loaded before you left the house.

Week 1 — no triggers, no pulling

Rebuild the walk from scratch. Deliberately avoid other dogs; zero reactive episodes is the win condition. - Daily: 5 min marker training indoors (mark, then treat, 20 reps — the marker is a contract: if you use it, you must reward, even accidentally), then 10 min loose-leash work somewhere empty. Leash slack = you walk. Leash tight = you stop dead or turn around. He never gets forward progress from a tight leash. - 5 emergency U-turn reps per walk with no dog present, so it's automatic before you need it. - Weekend: one 45-min long-line decompression sniffing walk somewhere wide open. Not a training session — sniffing lowers arousal. - Trigger journal every day. Identify the specific triggers — they can be very specific, like men with beards or dogs bigger than yours — and a journal is a useful way to pinpoint them. Record distance, what he did, recovery time, and whether the body language read fear or frustration. Bring this to the consult. - Advance when: 10 minutes of slack leash in a quiet place, and he turns to you when you mark.

Week 2 — find the threshold, start Look At That

The threshold is the point at which a trigger produces a behaviour change — distance is the key factor, though not the only one. Sit somewhere dogs pass predictably, start 100+ feet out, and close in only until you find the distance where he sees a dog and does not react. Write it down. Expect 40–80 feet, i.e. much further than feels reasonable.

Then run LAT, 3–4 sessions, 10 minutes max, always at or beyond that distance: start under threshold — the moment he spots the trigger without reacting, mark and treat; he should turn toward you in anticipation, and if he does, repeat 10–15 times. If he doesn't turn to you, either the marker isn't understood in that environment or you're too close, so increase the distance. Once he's consistently looking at the trigger without reacting, start marking him giving you eye contact instead. If he goes over threshold and reacts, just increase the distance with an emergency U-turn until he's no longer upset, then carry on. No scolding, no yanking.

Week 0 — find the threshold number

Everything is calibrated off this. Park where you can see dogs from a distance and control it. Back off until he can see another dog, notice it, and still take a treat without barking. That's your threshold. Under threshold means he's far enough away that he can notice the trigger without losing his brain. Dogs over threshold aren't in a space where they're able to learn — it's your responsibility to change the environment so they can succeed, which usually means starting at a very large distance. If he won't eat, you're too close.

Log: threshold distance, reactions per walk, recovery time, and whether he looks frustrated (loose, wiggly, straining forward) or fearful (stiff, backing up, hackles).

Week 1 — Management only

Goal: he rehearses the barking ZERO times this week. Every reaction is a rep that strengthens the behaviour. You're not fixing it yet; you're stopping the practice.

Walks: quiet industrial streets, school grounds after hours, empty office parks. Evenings actually help you here. Cross the street early, don't test him, no dog parks.

Indoors, 2 × 5 min/day: 1. Charge the marker — 20 reps of click-then-treat. 2. 1-2-3 Pattern Game. Developed as part of Leslie McDevitt's Control Unleashed program, it creates a predictable pattern: when the dog hears "3," a treat happens in a particular position, typically right by the handler's leg. No leash needed indoors — in a calm neutral tone say "1… 2… 3," about one number per second, and on "3" deliver the treat. This becomes your portable get-past-that-dog tool. 3. U-turn on cue — "let's go," 180° turn, treat. Your emergency exit for weeks 2–4. 4. Loose-leash in the driveway — treat every 2–3 steps on a slack leash; leash tightens, you stop walking. The rate feels absurd. It's correct.

Move on when: he eats readily at threshold distance and 1-2-3 works indoors.

Week 2 — Engage-Disengage, stage 1

Goal: change what another dog predicts. Right now another dog predicts conflict. You're making it predict chicken.

The engage-disengage game teaches a reactive dog that noticing a trigger predicts a reward, not an explosion, in three stages: marker for looking, marker for looking-then-back-to-you, and full self-disengagement.

2–3 sessions of 10–15 min at threshold distance or further — never closer. Daily walks stay management-only.

  • The instant he looks at another dog: mark and feed.
  • If progress stalls at stage 2, the most common cause is late marker timing — the mark must come within 0.5 seconds of the look.
  • You are not asking him to look at you or ignore the dog. Looking at the dog is what you're paying for.
  • The goal is to set the dog up to look at the trigger without reacting, and click/treat the second they do.

If he reacts: you were too close. Diagnostically that means the distance was too tight — drop well back and rebuild from stage 1. Add 20–30 feet next session.

Move on when: 8/10 reps he looks and swings back to you. Most owners see their first calm pass around week 2 when working below threshold.

Week 3 — Stage 2, then close the distance

Goal: he checks in without being prompted.

Don't mark the look. Wait. He looks at the dog, then voluntarily turns back to you — that's when you mark. He's making the decision, not obeying a cue. This builds new neural pathways for self-regulation; consistently reinforcing calm engagement and voluntary disengagement diminishes the automatic reactive response over time.

The distance rule: only reduce after 8/10 clean reps. Cut by 10–20%, not half. 50ft → 42ft, not 25ft. Any reaction means you moved too fast. Expect zig-zag, not a straight line.

Loose-leash: fade to intermittent reinforcement. Add "earn the grass" — 20 steps of slack leash buys a sniff break. Sniffing is a real reward and costs you nothing.

Full detail is in the five week-by-week subtasks. The governing rules: distance is the dial you always turn first, change one variable at a time, and end every session while he's still winning.

Rules: pick two quiet routes at two low-traffic times. Accept that for four weeks walks are training, not exercise. An under-stimulated collie has a lower reaction threshold.

Week 1 — baseline and mechanics indoors

Two 5–10 minute sessions a day, indoors or in the yard, no dogs anywhere. Mark and feed the instant the leash goes slack, feeding at your trouser seam — where you feed is where he learns to be. Build in chunks of five loose-leash steps ("300 Peck"), and add figure-eights around two objects, rewarding at your side whenever the leash is slack.

Meanwhile, gather two numbers: whether he pulls constantly or only near dogs, and his threshold distance — how many feet out he stiffens and stares, versus where he starts barking. Write it down; week 4 depends on it. He is already too close to learn if he cannot take food, cannot respond to a familiar cue, has a hard stare, or is leaning into the leash. On walks this week: manage only. Spot a dog, U-turn early, feed as you go.

Advance when: five loose-leash steps repeat reliably indoors, and the threshold number is written down.

Week 2 — Engage-Disengage, Level 1

Three or four sessions, 5–10 minutes each, parked at the far edge of somewhere dogs pass. Start at a distance where he's under threshold — not reacting and able to take treats — click the moment he sees the trigger, and deliver the treat where he has to turn his head toward you to eat it. Yes, you're rewarding him for looking at the dog; that's the point. These observation games use counter-conditioning to help the dog feel calmer, then reinforce better responses like looking at you rather than barking and lunging.

If he reacts or doesn't turn back after the click, move further away and reset at an easier distance. The bar: succeed with at least 3–5 reps in a row at the same distance before moving on, where a successful rep is turning back to you immediately after the click. And keep working Level 1 until he's calmly noticed the trigger from every direction. Note that dogs' focus tends to fade after about 30 minutes of intense training — a reactive dog's is far shorter, so five good minutes beats twenty that end in a bark.

Advance when: 3–5 clean reps in a row at your working distance, on two different days, no barking.

Week 3 — Level 2, and close the gap

Now let him notice the dog and say nothing: wait 1–5 seconds to see whether he offers to look away on his own, and pay that generously — the voluntary look-away is the behaviour you actually want on a real walk. If he's still fixated at 3–5 seconds, drop back to Level 1 rather than waiting him up into a reaction.

Expect to go backwards at some point this week — that's normal. Add wide-arc passing combined with the U-turn: dog appears, mark, feed, turn and leave before he tips. 3. The first four weeks

Two protocols running in parallel: loose-leash mechanics (daily, low stakes) and counter-conditioning to dogs (3–4 short sessions a week, always under threshold). Every week is broken out as its own subtask with the full detail.

Rules that start on day one and never stop

  • Never let the leash go tight while he's looking at a dog. The leash stops a fearful dog fleeing, so it switches to "fight" instead, and for a frustrated dog it blocks what he wants and adds to the frustration; your own stress also travels down a taut leash, so keep it loose and breathe slowly.
  • Cross the street or U-turn early, every time. No head-on passes on narrow pavements.
  • Walk at off-peak hours for the next month. Fewer dogs = fewer rehearsals.
  • Watch for trigger stacking. Lots of small triggers accumulate — each one alone wouldn't tip him over, but piled up they do. A bad session usually means he was already loaded before you left the house.

Week 1 — no triggers, no pulling

Rebuild the walk from scratch. Deliberately avoid other dogs; zero reactive episodes is the win condition. - Daily: 5 min marker training indoors (mark, then treat, 20 reps — the marker is a contract: if you use it, you must reward, even accidentally), then 10 min loose-leash work somewhere empty. Leash slack = you walk. Leash tight = you stop dead or turn around. He never gets forward progress from a tight leash. - 5 emergency U-turn reps per walk with no dog present, so it's automatic before you need it. - Weekend: one 45-min long-line decompression sniffing walk somewhere wide open. Not a training session — sniffing lowers arousal. - Trigger journal every day. Identify the specific triggers — they can be very specific, like men with beards or dogs bigger than yours — and a journal is a useful way to pinpoint them. Record distance, what he did, recovery time, and whether the body language read fear or frustration. Bring this to the consult. - Advance when: 10 minutes of slack leash in a quiet place, and he turns to you when you mark.

Week 2 — find the threshold, start Look At That

The threshold is the point at which a trigger produces a behaviour change — distance is the key factor, though not the only one. Sit somewhere dogs pass predictably, start 100+ feet out, and close in only until you find the distance where he sees a dog and does not react. Write it down. Expect 40–80 feet, i.e. much further than feels reasonable.

Then run LAT, 3–4 sessions, 10 minutes max, always at or beyond that distance: start under threshold — the moment he spots the trigger without reacting, mark and treat; he should turn toward you in anticipation, and if he does, repeat 10–15 times. If he doesn't turn to you, either the marker isn't understood in that environment or you're too close, so increase the distance. Once he's consistently looking at the trigger without reacting, start marking him giving you eye contact instead. If he goes over threshold and reacts, just increase the distance with an emergency U-turn until he's no longer upset, then carry on. No scolding, no yanking.

Don't pet him during this work — you can't reinforce fear by coddling, but it may make him feel more trapped or lead him to redirect onto you.

Do not close the distance this week.

If it turns out to be frustration rather than fear: LAT still applies because it teaches a better response, but avoid physically holding him back as that increases frustration and risks a redirection bite — and add an appropriate outlet, since a dog who lunges because he wants to play needs somewhere legitimate to do that, alongside a physically and mentally enriching life that lowers his baseline. Plus impulse control: sit-and-wait at every doorway and kerb.

Advance when: 8 of 10 sightings at threshold produce look-then-check-in with no barking.

Week 3 — shrink the distance, add movement

  • Decrease the distance gradually, a foot at a time, keeping the marker and treats going as long as he doesn't react — and be prepared to go back to marking him looking at the trigger and work up to eye contact again. Two reactions at a new distance means you go back to the last one that worked.
  • Parallel walking — the highest-value exercise of the month. Recruit a friend with a calm dog. Walk both in the same direction, wide apart, never face to face, narrowing the gap across several sessions. You control the other dog, which you never do with strangers.
  • Add the 1-2-3 pattern game for ambushes you can't avoid: count aloud while walking, feed on "three", repeat. Gives him a job and keeps him moving past instead of fixating.
  • Start fading food on loose-leash walking only. Keep paying generously for dog-sightings.
  • Advance when: he holds it together with a moving dog at roughly half the Week 2 distance.

Week 4 — generalise, then decide

  • Short 10-min sessions in 3–4 new locations (different neighbourhood, evening car park, outside a dog park, quiet downtown pavement). Dogs don't generalise; reset to a generous distance in each new place.
  • One normal evening walk on your usual route with the management rules still on. Count episodes; compare with Week 1.
  • Loose leash should now hold for a full 15 minutes on intermittent food, with one added distraction per session.

End-of-month review, journal in hand: threshold distance Week 1 vs now (halved or better = it's working); episodes per walk (trending down); recovery time (often improves before the barking does). Week 1 — Stop the backsliding

Management, all week: walk at low-traffic times. Pick routes with escape options — wide streets, cul-de-sacs, empty lots. Avoid narrow sidewalks and the dog park perimeter entirely. Practise fast U-turns as a way of luring the dog away from a distraction — ten reps on a quiet street with no dog present, so it's automatic when you need it. If a dog appears too close: turn, cross, or step behind a parked car and feed until it's gone. That's the plan, not cheating.

Baseline measurement (once, 15 min): find the distance at which your dog notices another dog but can still take a treat and look back at you. Usually at least 20 feet, though some dogs need considerably more — a leash-reactive border collie might start triggering at anything closer than 50 feet, meaning you'd begin work at 60. Write the number down. It's your progress metric for four weeks.

Attention foundation (5 min/day indoors): charge a marker word ("yes" → treat, 20x), name response (10 reps), hand target (10 reps).

Advance when: you know your threshold in feet, the dog turns to its name indoors, and you got through the week with fewer than two full reactions.

Week 2 — Leash mechanics + Engage/Disengage

Track A — loose leash (10 min, 5×/wk, driveway or empty lot). The rule doing most of the work: tight leash means forward motion stops, every time, including when you're in a hurry. Become a tree; move again only when the leash goes slack. Feed at your seam, not out in front — where you feed is where the dog will want to be. Count steps of slack leash and reset to one on any pull. Change direction every 15–20 seconds so the dog tracks you instead of towing you.

Split your walks: a short strict "training walk" and a long-line "sniff walk" with different rules. Enforcing loose-leash on every outing for a collie will burn you both out.

Track B — Engage/Disengage (10–15 min, 3×/wk), at or beyond your threshold distance. You sit and watch dogs; you don't walk past them. The critical constraint is that you only play this when the dog is under threshold.

  • Stage 1: dog looks at the other dog → mark the instant it looks → feed. You're paying for noticing calmly.
  • Stage 2: once that's fluent, wait a beat and mark only when the dog looks and then turns back to you on its own. This is the sequence — rewarding glances at dogs, then voluntary disengagement.

Work in roughly five-minute increments, then take a break and let the dog relax. Even sub-threshold exposure accumulates stress, so keep sessions brief and positive.

Too close looks like: can't take the treat, hard stare, stiff body, won't break the look. Add 10–15 feet immediately. When unsure, always add distance.

Advance when: 10–15 slack-leash steps in a quiet place, and the dog comes back to you after looking at a dog at least half the time.

Week 3 — Close the distance, add movement

Only advance if Week 2's criteria were actually met. This is not a schedule you owe anyone.

  • Move 5–10 feet closer. One variable at a time.
  • Add movement: walk parallel to the trigger rather than sitting still. Head-on approaches are the hardest thing you'll ever ask for and are months away, not this month.
  • Pattern game "1-2-3" — say the count, feed on three, walking as you go. Gives the dog a predictable rhythm and gives you something to do with your voice other than tense up.
  • Move the leash work to a new but still dog-free location. Expect performance to halve. That's context specificity, not disobedience — drop criteria, pay more, rebuild.
  • Reward every unprompted check-in heavily. That becomes your default walking behaviour.
  • Once or twice, run a real 15-minute walk with both rule sets on. Bail early if it goes badly; ending on a bad rep is worse than a short walk.

Collie-specific, don't skip: 15 min/day of brain work outside the reactivity training — scent games, flirt pole, trick training, shaping, snuffle mat. An under-stimulated collie has a higher baseline arousal, and everything else you're doing is fighting that number.

Week 4 — Real walks and an honest reassessment

  • Three real walks on your normal routes with the full toolkit.
  • One deliberate closer pass per walk, with a bail-out ready. One.
  • Thin the treat rate on loose-leash walking to intermittent. Keep reactivity payments at full rate — that's emotional work, not obedience.
  • Re-measure the threshold distance exactly as in Week 1. Compare.

What normal progress actually looks like: not a cured dog. Shorter threshold distance, much faster recovery after a reaction (seconds, not the rest of the walk), less intense reactions, and you spotting triggers before the dog does. Reactivity is a months-to-a-year project. Four weeks buys you a manageable walk.

3. The first four weeks

The engine is the Engage/Disengage game (Alice Tong, KPA CTP). It's designed for dogs that respond with a "fight" or "fool around" response — dogs who become over-aroused quickly and hurl themselves toward the trigger out of fear, anxiety or frustration, and who have immense difficulty disengaging in order to self-interrupt. That is your dog. There are two levels: the first rewards engaging with the trigger while not showing the stress response, building positive feelings; the second rewards turning away — a behaviour incompatible with barking at or pulling toward the trigger.

Buy first: front-clip harness · 6 ft non-retractable leash · treat pouch · high-value moist treats · clicker · bathmat. The protocol's own supply list is high-value treats, a clicker, and a humane harness or collar.

Week 1 — Measure, and stop the bleeding. No trigger training.

Before starting a training plan, use management to decrease how often he's reacting: every time he practises an undesired behaviour outside of training time he goes backward in training and continues to feel stressed around the trigger.

  • Write down his threshold in feet. Expect two numbers. The example the vets use: barking at 30 ft from a calm dog, 50 ft from an excited or reactive one. Below-threshold means far enough away that he doesn't react — that could be 10 feet or 40, and different triggers have different distances.
  • Classify frustration vs fear from his body language (see above). Bring the note to class.
  • Re-route and re-time walks to low-dog-density: early morning, late evening, quiet streets, open ground where you see dogs at 100 ft.
  • Drill the emergency U-turn with no dog present. Practise fast U-turns by luring with a treat near or on his nose, or by tossing "find it" treats on the ground as you both change direction quickly.
  • Loose-leash foundations indoors and in the yard only.
  • Give the collie an outlet — long-line sniffing plus ~10 min/day of brain work. Unmet needs raise baseline arousal, which lowers his threshold for everything.

Marker: threshold known in feet · type identified · ≤2 blow-ups all week · U-turn works dry.

Week 2 — Engage/Disengage Level 1. Do not close distance.

Start at a distance where he isn't reacting; be quiet and still so he notices the trigger on his own. At the precise moment he ENGAGES by looking at the trigger, click. When he turns his head toward you after the click, feed. If he reacts or doesn't turn back after the click, move further away and reset at an easier distance.

Goal: at least 3–5 repetitions in a row at the same distance, a successful rep being that he turns back to you immediately after the click. Play for 1–5 minutes, then take a break; repeat only if he's ready and eager. If the trigger is moving or changing intensity, keep playing Level 1 until he has calmly engaged with it from every direction before moving on.

Separately: 10 min loose-leash on a quiet dog-free street, then free sniffing. Stop dead when the leash tightens; move when it slackens.

Marker: 3–5 clean Level 1 reps in a row, on two separate outings.

Week 3 — Level 2 (disengage), close distance in steps

Let him notice the trigger, then wait 1–5 seconds to see if he offers to look away on his own — if he fixates longer than 5 seconds, go back to Level 1. At the precise moment he DISENGAGES by looking away, click, then feed. After 3–5 successful reps in a row, move 1–5 steps closer. Steps, not yards — this is where everyone rushes. If the trigger is moving or changing in intensity, go back to Level 1 at the new distance.

Also build one incompatible behaviour on cue. Teach it in a quiet environment without distractions and pile on the treats, then build distractions in, then add the trigger at a below-threshold distance and ask for the replacement behaviour — eventually he sees the trigger as a cue to do the new behaviour and earn a reward instead of lunging.

Marker: threshold measurably shrunk (5 ft counts) · one clean Level 2 rep with a moving dog.

Week 4 — Generalise, then rehearse the pass

Duplicate the entire procedure with a new dog, and repeat with as many examples of the trigger as necessary to convince him that his trigger makes great things happen — so that when he sees any dog, he looks to you for a reward instead of lunging. Big, small, fluffy, barky. A dog who's fine with the neighbour's beagle and hopeless otherwise simply hasn't generalised yet.

Then deliberately practise the pass while you still have an escape hatch: cross the street early, put a parked car between you, U-turn when ambushed. Those are the skill, not failures.

Set the target honestly. In many cases, working up to walking past another dog calmly on leash is a perfectly acceptable goal. He may not be destined to be a dog-park dog — some dogs prefer human company or have more introverted social personalities, and a busy dog park can be like a concert he has no interest in attending. Aim for passes calmly, not makes friends.

  1. Punishing the barking. Suppresses the moment, not the feeling. Often worsens it.
  2. Working too close. If he won't take cheese, he's over threshold and cannot learn. Distance is the dial.

  4. Only training on normal walks, where dogs ambush you, instead of setups you control.
  5. Sessions too long. One to five minutes.
  6. Closing distance too fast. If he reverts to lunging, you've moved too far, too fast.
  7. Skipping management, so he rehearses reactions five times a walk and undoes the training.
  8. Mixing leash drills into reactivity sessions.
  9. Retractable leash or neck collar on a hard puller.
  10. Stopping walks altogether. It's tempting, but then he isn't getting the mental stimulation and physical exercise he needs.

Lunging on leash can usually be modified into more acceptable behaviour; at minimum, good management minimises the outbursts. It takes patience and dedication to the protocols, but walks can eventually be stress-free for both of you.

3. The first four weeks

Equipment first (~$60–90)

  • Front-clip no-pull harness. Front-clipping harnesses cause your dog to turn back and look at you, encouraging check-ins and discouraging pulling — without causing pain.
  • 6 ft flat nylon or leather leash. No retractable.
  • Waist treat pouch — you cannot run this out of a ziplock in your pocket.
  • Optional: head halter (Gentle Leader / Halti). A head halter can be very helpful for managing leash-reactive dogs.
  • Treats: pea-sized, high value, things she doesn't usually get — cheese, chicken, hot dog or liver. Buy far more than feels reasonable.

Rules that apply to all four weeks

  • Separate skill sessions from walks. Skill sessions: 5–10 min, twice a day, low distraction, house or driveway. Walks: treat them as a test, not a lesson. If you only have time for one, do the skill session.
  • Walk off-peak or drive somewhere quiet. Walking at off-peak hours or driving to a quieter location prevents the rehearsal of unwanted behaviour, and when a dog does appear, move between parked cars, cross the road, or do a U-turn to get out of the way. Preventing a reaction is training.
  • Drain the tank first. A 14-month collie mix who's under-stimulated is a more reactive collie mix. Ten minutes of sniffing, food puzzles or a flirt pole before a walk lowers the baseline.
  • Never punish the barking. With frustration-based reactivity, making the sight of another dog predict something unpleasant makes it worse.

Week 1 — measure, manage, build two skills

Goal: stop making it worse and find out what you're dealing with. Expect zero visible progress on walks.

  1. Fit the harness; two short in-house sessions with treats before its first walk.
  2. Measure her threshold distance on 3–4 walks: the distance where she first notices a dog but hasn't yet barked. Typically 30–60 ft. Write it down — this number is your only real progress metric.
  3. Skill A: "Find it." Say it, toss 3–5 treats on the ground, let her hoover. 20 reps, 2x/day. This is your emergency exit — it moves her, drops her head, and puts her nose to work. Tossing a handful of treats on the ground both moves the dog away and engages her nose.
  4. Skill B: "Let's go" U-turn. Say it, pivot 180°, walk off briskly, feed as she catches up. 10 reps, 2x/day, with no dog in sight, so it's automatic when there is one.
  5. On walks: at threshold+10 ft, don't train — cross the road or U-turn, feeding throughout.

Done when: you know her number, she moves normally in the harness, and both cues work in the driveway.

Week 2 — loose leash on empty ground + first helper-dog session

Goal: pulling starts to shift; reactivity work begins in a rigged setup.

  3. Weekend: the helper-dog session. With one calm, neutral dog: start with the helper about 10 ft beyond her threshold — if her threshold is 40 ft, put them at 50 ft. Have the helper bring their dog into view from behind a gate, hedge, parked car or corner. The moment she notices but before she can overreact, feed 10–15 treats in a row with calm praise; after about five seconds the helper leads their dog out of sight, and the treats and praise stop the instant it disappears. Wait a couple of minutes, repeat 5–8 times, stop. Twice this weekend if you can.

Done when: 20+ steps of loose leash on empty ground, and in the rigged setup she sees the helper dog appear and immediately looks to you, wagging in anticipation of her treats. That look-back is the thing you're actually buying.

3. The first four weeks

Two tracks in parallel: loose-leash mechanics trained where it's easy, and desensitisation + counter-conditioning for the barking run at a measured distance. The organising number is your threshold distance — week 1 measures it, weeks 2–4 shrink it.

Gear first: front-clip harness (front-clip harnesses distribute force more evenly and reduce the risk of tracheal or spinal damage, but should be used alongside ongoing training rather than as a solution on their own); 6 ft leash; 15–30 ft long line, not retractable; waist treat pouch; and high-value soft, smelly treats — turkey hot dog, string cheese, chicken breast, pea-sized. Kibble will not hold up at threshold.

Week 1 — measure, and stop rehearsing

Goal: find your threshold number and get to zero barking episodes. You are not fixing anything yet.

  • Threshold test, twice. Somewhere with dogs at a distance — park edge, wide parking lot. Start absurdly far; for most leash-reactive dogs the starting threshold is 50–100 feet from another dog. Feed a treat every time the dog notices a dog. Step 10 ft closer. Repeat. The distance where the dog stops taking food is your threshold. Write the number down.
  • Every walk, stay outside that distance. Cross the street, U-turn early, duck down a driveway. Score each walk 0–3 for barking episodes. Aim for zero.
  • Weekend: one long-line decompression sniff walk somewhere empty (fire road, school field at dawn) plus 10 minutes of a brain job — scatter feeding, snuffle mat, a new trick. Not optional for a herding mix; arousal management is half the treatment.
  • All month: keep the leash loose when you see a dog. Your tension goes straight down the leash.

Advance when: you have your number, and 4+ consecutive walks at zero barking episodes.

Week 2 — mechanics in the driveway, and start Engage-Disengage

A. Loose leash, 2 × 10 min/evening, no dogs present. Start with the Box Step — a tiny step to the right; as long as the leash clasp is hanging down and the leash is loose, click/treat. Then a tiny step left, click/treat. A step back, click/treat. A step forward, click/treat. Before long the dog decides staying very near is quite fabulous, and you transition to two or three steps forward, taking treats at your knee. Every treat is delivered at your knee, never out front — treat placement is what builds position.

Then build duration with the 300 peck method: 1 step then treat, 2 steps then treat, 3 steps, counting up. Building distance step by step, in chunks, while the dog keeps the leash loose and maintains position at your side. Pull = go back to 1 and count again. Plus penalty yards: the moment the leash goes tight, back up or turn and go the other direction. Pulling never gets to move forward. Not once. Consistency matters more than technique here.

B. Engage-Disengage stage 1, 3 sessions of 5–10 min at threshold distance. The game teaches the dog that noticing a trigger predicts a reward rather than an explosion, in three stages: marker for looking, marker for looking-then-back-to-you, and full self-disengagement. Stage 1: the moment the dog looks at another dog, mark and treat. Pay for noticing, ask for nothing. This is the desensitisation-and-counter-conditioning part — it teaches the dog to associate the presence of the trigger with treats instead of fear, and to look up at you for guidance instead of barking and lunging. Stop the instant the dog can't take food. Five good reps beat twenty sloppy ones.

Advance when: 5 clean driveway steps, and 5+ unprompted head-swings back to you at threshold.

Week 3 — close the distance

A. Engage-Disengage stage 2, 4 sessions. At first you mark the instant the dog looks at the trigger; later you wait half a second to see if the dog chooses to turn back to you on their own. Hand the decision to the dog. Can't turn back? Add 10 ft, drop to stage 1. Reduce distance only 5–10 ft at a time, only after two clean sessions. A bark means you moved too fast — add the distance back.

B. Loose leash goes mobile — quiet street, quiet time. Expect it to fall apart; reset to 1–2 steps between treats outdoors and rebuild. Penalty yards still apply every time.

C. Teach the emergency U-turn, 5 min/day, no dogs around. Cue ("this way!"), turn 180, jog 3–4 steps away, jackpot with three treats. Make it a game before you need it. A dog appearing around a corner is a when, not an if — and a plan that only works in clean conditions isn't enough when a dog suddenly appears.

D. One real pass at week's end, on a wide street, feeding continuously the whole time the other dog is in view. Most owners see their first calm pass around week 2 if they're working below threshold.

Advance when: working distance is down at least a third from your week 1 number, and the U-turn works on cue.

Week 4 — real-world passes, then decide

  • 4–5 structured passes this week. Routes where you control distance: wide streets, open parks, big lots. One pass per outing at first, then two or three. End every outing on a success — don't go hunting one more dog.
  • Keep the scorecard. Two columns on your phone, one line per walk: reactions 0–3, and the closest distance handled calmly.
  • Pulling unchanged after three consistent weeks on the front-clip? A head halter is the next step for dogs whose pulling hasn't improved on a front-clip after three consistent weeks, or reactive dogs needing head-steering control during trigger moments — but keep the leash loose with no pop corrections; the redirect should be gentle and continuous, not sudden.

Then answer three questions: Has the working distance shrunk? Does the dog recover faster even if reactions still happen? Is pulling better on a loose-leash-only route? (Recovery speed improves before frequency does. Any shrink at all means the protocol is working.)

And choose: - Clear progress, and you enjoyed it → repeat weeks 3–4 for another month. You may not need to hire anyone. - Some progress but stuck at the same distance, or you're not confident reading the dog → enroll (D For Dogz $595, or HSSV L1 $300). Most likely outcome, and a good one. - No progress, or growling/snapping/redirecting appeared → private behaviour work, drop the group-class plan.

Four weeks is a checkpoint, not a finish line. Reactivity work is measured in months. The point of week 4 is knowing you're on the right road.

3. The first four weeks

Equipment first (~$60–90)

  • Front-clip no-pull harness. Front-clipping harnesses cause your dog to turn back and look at you, encouraging check-ins and discouraging pulling — without causing pain.
  • 6 ft flat nylon or leather leash. No retractable.
  • Waist treat pouch — you cannot run this out of a ziplock in your pocket.
  • Optional: head halter (Gentle Leader / Halti). A head halter can be very helpful for managing leash-reactive dogs.
  • Treats: pea-sized, high value, things she doesn't usually get — cheese, chicken, hot dog or liver. Buy far more than feels reasonable.

Rules that apply to all four weeks

  • Separate skill sessions from walks. Skill sessions: 5–10 min, twice a day, low distraction, house or driveway. Walks: treat them as a test, not a lesson. If you only have time for one, do the skill session.
  • Walk off-peak or drive somewhere quiet. Walking at off-peak hours or driving to a quieter location prevents the rehearsal of unwanted behaviour, and when a dog does appear, move between parked cars, cross the road, or do a U-turn to get out of the way. Preventing a reaction is training.
  • Drain the tank first. A 14-month collie mix who's under-stimulated is a more reactive collie mix. Ten minutes of sniffing, food puzzles or a flirt pole before a walk lowers the baseline.
  • Never punish the barking. With frustration-based reactivity, making the sight of another dog predict something unpleasant makes it worse.

Week 1 — measure, manage, build two skills

Goal: stop making it worse and find out what you're dealing with. Expect zero visible progress on walks.

  1. Fit the harness; two short in-house sessions with treats before its first walk.
  2. Measure her threshold distance on 3–4 walks: the distance where she first notices a dog but hasn't yet barked. Typically 30–60 ft. Write it down — this number is your only real progress metric.
  3. Skill A: "Find it." Say it, toss 3–5 treats on the ground, let her hoover. 20 reps, 2x/day. This is your emergency exit — it moves her, drops her head, and puts her nose to work. Tossing a handful of treats on the ground both moves the dog away and engages her nose.
  4. Skill B: "Let's go" U-turn. Say it, pivot 180°, walk off briskly, feed as she catches up. 10 reps, 2x/day, with no dog in sight, so it's automatic when there is one.
  5. On walks: at threshold+10 ft, don't train — cross the road or U-turn, feeding throughout.

Done when: you know her number, she moves normally in the harness, and both cues work in the driveway.

Goal: pulling starts to shift; reactivity work begins in a rigged setup.

  2. Reward position, not just the absence of pulling — a treat every 3–5 steps while she's beside you on a loose leash.
  3. Weekend: the helper-dog session. With one calm, neutral dog: start with the helper about 10 ft beyond her threshold — if her threshold is 40 ft, put them at 50 ft. Have the helper bring their dog into view from behind a gate, hedge, parked car or corner. The moment she notices but before she can overreact, feed 10–15 treats in a row with calm praise; after about five seconds the helper leads their dog out of sight, and the treats and praise stop the instant it disappears. Wait a couple of minutes, repeat 5–8 times, stop. Twice this weekend if you can.
  4. Read how she takes food. If she's snatching, or you feel teeth bearing down on your fingers, her arousal is too high — add distance.

Done when: 20+ steps of loose leash on empty ground, and in the rigged setup she sees the helper dog appear and immediately looks to you, wagging in anticipation of her treats. That look-back is the thing you're actually buying.

  1. Keep the weekend helper sessions, closing distance in small increments — a few feet per block of reps, not per rep. Progress more gradually than you think you should; there's no disadvantage to over-training the happy response, but you risk a setback every time you push too far and she reacts.
  2. Add movement: once she's steady with the helper stationary, start walking your dog too, slowly decreasing the distance until the two can pass on the sidewalk. Parallel first, converge later.
  3. Apply it on real walks. Spot a dog at threshold+ distance → stop, get her attention, feed continuously while it passes, release. If she won't eat, you were too close: "let's go" U-turn, add 15 ft next time.
  4. Move loose-leash work to a quiet residential street at off-peak hour. It will fall apart the first time — go back to stopping dead every time the leash tightens.

Done when: her threshold number is measurably smaller than week 1, and you can hold her attention while a dog passes across the street.

  3. Thin the treat rate on loose leash only (every 8–10 steps instead of 3–5). Do not thin it on dog sightings — that stays 1:1 for months yet.
