# Training Plan — Rescue Border Collie Mix, 14 Months

**Presenting problems:** hard leash pulling; barks at every dog on walks. South Bay CA, evenings + weekends.

---

## 1. Hire a trainer, or DIY?

**Recommendation: DIY the four-week plan, with one $300 class booked in parallel as a safety net. Total ~$360.**

Not full DIY. Not a $2,400 private program.

**Why DIY is appropriate here:**

- 14 months is adolescence. Pulling plus barking at every dog is the single most common adolescent presentation there is — over-arousal, not a rare or dangerous case. Nothing in your brief mentions a bite, snap, or injury.
- Both problems have published, step-by-step protocols you can execute yourself. This isn't a case that needs professional diagnosis before anything can begin.
- The binding constraint is **your consistency, not your knowledge.** Certified behaviour consultant Rachel Lane's position is blunt: <cite index="106-2">there are no quick fixes, and an owner's participation, consistency, and follow-through all play a critical role in a successful behaviour modification plan.</cite> A trainer cannot supply that for you. $2,400 spent followed by inconsistent practice beats $0 and daily practice — never.
- Evenings-and-weekends actually **suits** DIY. This protocol wants many short sessions of one to five minutes, not one long weekly appointment.

**Why not pure DIY — book the class too:**

Reactivity isn't static, so it shouldn't be left to drift. <cite index="106-0">Reactivity puts a dog at high risk of acting aggressively because they're in a heightened emotional state which shuts down the thinking parts of the brain — a reactive dog is more likely to behave instinctively with fight, flight, or freeze than to consider their actions.</cite> And per Lane, <cite index="106-1">a dog that has never injured anyone can still have these behaviours morph into defensive aggression over time, after many failed attempts to create distance from a stimulus.</cite>

$300 buys professional eyes on the case cheaply. If the instructor says "this is more than frustration," you learn that in week one rather than month six.

**What a trainer genuinely adds** (worth being honest about): clicker timing you can't self-assess; reading the dog's early stress signals; accurate threshold judgement — most owners work far too close and wonder why nothing changes; and accountability.

### Go straight to a professional and skip DIY if any of these are true

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| Any bite, snap, or mouth contact — even without injury | Private trainer or vet behaviourist. Also disqualifies self-enrolment in the group class. |
| Barking is **fear**-based (see the test below) | Private trainer |
| You can't physically hold the dog mid-outburst | Get help now — safety issue |
| Escalating week over week | Private trainer |
| Behaviour appeared **suddenly** | **Vet first.** Pain is a commonly overlooked cause of behaviour change. |
| Four weeks of consistent work, zero change | Private trainer |

### The test that decides everything — do this on your next walk

The word "reactivity" hides two completely different problems. <cite index="106-6">Fear is a common motivator, but there might be a different underlying emotion, such as frustration or excitement; the important point is that a reactive dog responds faster and more intensely than a non-reactive dog.</cite>

- <cite index="106-7">Dogs that become frustrated when their excitement to greet another dog is thwarted by a leash or barrier often lack emotional self-control.</cite> → **Frustrated greeter.** Strains *forward*, tail high, bouncy, recovers in ~30 seconds. **DIY plan applies.**
- <cite index="106-8,106-9">Anxious dogs are often reactive due to lack of socialisation as a puppy, or a previous bad encounter such as being attacked, which can create fear later in life.</cite> Here the goal of the behaviour <cite index="106-1">is often to increase the distance between the dog and the perceived threat.</cite> → **Fear.** Backs *away*, weight back, hackles, stiff, hard stare, slow to settle. **Hire someone.**

Given a 14-month-old barking at *every* dog while pulling hard toward it, frustration is the likely answer — but confirm it before spending money.

## Non-negotiables

- **Never punish the barking.** <cite index="131-18">Science and research in dog training have revealed that intimidation, pain, or the threat of pain are simply not necessary and cause more problems training an aggressive, reactive, or fearful dog.</cite>
- **Consistency is the whole game.** <cite index="126-2">For this method to work you must do it every time your dog pulls — if they're allowed to pull on some occasions, it will only continue.</cite> <cite index="132-17">If you never let the leash get tight, your dog won't learn that he can pull you.</cite>
- **Going slow is the fast route.** <cite index="131-14">Keeping your dog under threshold will actually help him learn faster and give you longer-term results.</cite>
- **Learn the body language.** <cite index="106-5">If you catch subtle signs like lip-licking or pinning back the ears that indicate your dog's emotions are shifting, you can intervene before your dog becomes reactive.</cite>
- **Medication isn't failure or a last resort.** <cite index="106-6">It can help calm a dog and therefore make them more receptive to learning new behaviour patterns.</cite> A vet conversation, and premature here — flagged only so it isn't misread later.

**Collie rider:** an under-stimulated 14-month collie mix is a more reactive collie. Keep the four weeks stocked with sniffing walks, food puzzles and short trick sessions. Arousal with nowhere to go lands on passing dogs.
