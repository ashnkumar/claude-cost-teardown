# You are choosing between two reports, on behalf of the person who asked for them.

Both reports answer the same question, for the same person, from the same prompt.
Your job is not to score them. It is to say **which one he would rather have
received**, dimension by dimension, and then overall.

This is a comparison, not a grading exercise, and the difference matters. An
earlier version of this task asked for absolute scores against anchored levels.
It failed in a specific, instructive way: both reports in a pair cleared every
anchor, scored identically on all nineteen items, and the comparison came out a
tie — while the same judge's written summary named four concrete differences that
all pointed the same way. **The information was there and the scale had nowhere
to put it.** So: no scores here. Pick a side.

## What he cares about, in his own words

These are verbatim from the person the reports are for, reacting to reports he
read blind. They are the standard. Where they conflict with generic
report-quality instincts, his win.

- On the options section: *"B doesn't have much detail on the local options — it
  only goes into detail on the recommended option. B's lack of detail on the
  local options is a killer."*
- On the week-by-week: *"The 4 week plan in B is way too sparse — not actionable
  at all."* · *"More detail in the week by week thing."* · *"A doesn't give
  threshold METRICS for each week on when I know to advance."*
- On deferring substance: *"This one is punting more info into the subtasks but I
  want the report to be comprehensive and THEN the subtasks encapsulate all that
  into actionable steps."*
- On what makes a report good to read: *"I really do think conciseness and
  matter-of-factness are important. How much information can I glean without
  having to read through so much stuff?"*
- On length specifically: *"It's not necessarily that longer is better. That may
  have happened to be the case because, in order to get the details I wanted, it
  ended up being longer. I would love those longer ones in a more concise way,
  with fewer words but keeping the density of information."*
- On formatting: *"It uses tables that make info clear — it shows me the
  credentials which is really helpful, the local options shows a blocker which is
  helpful, and consolidated recommendations."*
- On what he wants at the end: *"Gives me a full consolidated 'your next 3
  moves'."*

## Four rules that override your instincts

**1. Length is neither a virtue nor a vice.** You are given both character counts
as context for the density dimension ONLY. A report that says the same things in
half the words wins density. A report that is longer *because it carries more
decision-relevant detail* wins the content dimensions. Both happen. Do not let
the longer one win by default, and do not let the shorter one win by default.

**2. Every call needs a verbatim quote from each report.** The quotes are checked
programmatically as substrings. A dimension whose quotes do not match is
discarded and recorded as unusable. Do not paraphrase, do not fix typos, do not
stitch fragments together, do not add ellipses inside a quote.

**3. Count nothing. Ask what he would rather have.** "Report 1 has four options
and Report 2 has three" is not by itself an answer — he asked for two or three,
and a fourth thin option is worse than three good ones. The question is always
whether the difference would change what he does.

**4. EQUAL is available per dimension and forbidden overall.** If two reports are
genuinely indistinguishable on the four-week plan, say EQUAL. But on the OVERALL
call you must pick one, even when it is close, and say whether it is a clear or a
slight preference. A tie overall is the one answer that is never useful.

You are not told which model, settings, or configuration produced either report.
Nothing about how they were generated is relevant, and speculating about it is a
failure. Neither position (1 or 2) carries any meaning — the order is arbitrary
and gets swapped on a second pass to check you for position bias.

# The task both reports were answering, verbatim

> Rescue border collie mix, 14 months. Pulls hard on the leash and barks at every
> other dog on walks. South Bay, California; I can do evenings and weekends.
>
> Three things only:
> 1. Whether to hire a trainer or do it myself.
> 2. If hiring — two or three local options with real prices.
> 3. A week-by-week plan for the first four weeks.
>
> Don't research anything beyond those three.

The specifics available to both reports: **14 months old**, a **rescue**, a
**border collie mix** (so a working breed with real drive), **pulls hard**,
**barks at every other dog on walks** (the frequency is the tell — a threshold
problem, not a one-off), **South Bay California**, **evenings and weekends only**.

**"Don't research anything beyond those three" is part of the spec.** Material
outside the three asks is a defect, not a bonus — and it costs him reading time,
so it belongs in the density call as well. A fourth section on nutrition or crate
training is off-spec no matter how good it is.

# The five dimensions

For each one, name the winner, say whether the gap is CLEAR or SLIGHT, and quote
both sides.

## 1 · LOCAL OPTIONS — the one he weighted most heavily

He read eight reports blind and this is the dimension that decided the most
pairs. What he rewards, in rough order of how much he reacted to it:

- **Every option written up well enough to choose a different one.** The failure
  he called a killer is a report that writes up its recommendation properly and
  leaves the alternatives as one-liners. A report with three evenly-developed
  options beats one with a beautiful recommendation and two stubs.
- **Real prices, and what each price buys.** A figure with the number of sessions
  and the format beats a bare figure, which beats "contact for pricing".
- **Who the trainer actually is** — a certification, a stated method, a
  specialism in reactivity. He said credentials are "really helpful".
- **Blockers and disqualifiers.** He specifically valued being shown *why an
  option might not work for him*: a class that meets when he is at work, a
  waitlist, a prerequisite, aversive tools.
- **Not missing a local option that exists and fits.** He noticed this twice. It
  is real, and it is the smallest of these — it never flipped his ranking on its
  own.

## 2 · THE FOUR-WEEK PLAN

- **Executable without looking anything else up**: named drills with duration,
  frequency and setting, week by week. He complained twice about sparse plans.
- **Advance criteria that are observable.** He raised this unprompted, in both
  directions: *"doesn't give threshold METRICS for each week on when I know to
  advance."* A distance, a count, a behaviour you could watch happen. Vague
  encouragement to progress when ready does not count.
- **Fitted to his constraints** — evenings and weekends — and to a reactive dog,
  meaning the barking is handled as a threshold and management problem rather
  than instructions to walk into triggers.
- **Self-contained, not punted to the subtask list.**

## 3 · THE DECISION — hire or do it yourself

- An **unambiguous verdict** he could act on without re-reading.
- **Reasons tied to this dog**, not to dogs in general: the age, the rescue
  history, the breed's drive, the frequency of the barking, the time constraint.
- A **decision rule** — the conditions under which the answer flips, specific
  enough to check against reality later.
- **Gettable without reading the section.** He liked verdicts he could see at a
  glance.

Note: in the corpus this dimension separates reports least. Most competent
reports do this well. If they are genuinely equal here, say EQUAL rather than
manufacturing a difference — but read carefully first, because the *reasons* and
the *rule* vary much more than the verdict does.

## 4 · DENSITY — information gleaned per unit of reading

This is the dimension he added himself, after noticing it about reports he
otherwise liked. It is not "which is shorter".

- **Load-bearing text.** Before you decide this one, do the work: find the
  passages in each report you would delete without losing a single
  decision-relevant fact. Preamble, restating his own question back at him,
  encouragement, hedging, and the same fact given twice in different words all
  count. Quote up to three from each. The report with less deletable text wins.
- **Formatting that compresses rather than decorates.** A table that puts the
  options side by side, or the weeks at a glance, is doing real work. Prose
  carrying what a table would carry better is not. So much formatting that the
  content hides is its own failure.
- **A consolidated "do this now".** He named this as a positive twice — one
  block that collapses everything into the next few actions.
- **Matter-of-fact register.** He used the word "verbose" as a criticism.

## 5 · GROUNDING

The FACTS block gives you, for each report, the prices that were checked against
a provider ledger.

**Read the ledger's meaning correctly, because the obvious reading is wrong.** It
is a **verified whitelist of prices we confirmed on the provider's page, not a
catalogue of every price that exists.** A price that is not in the ledger is
UNVERIFIED — it is *not* wrong, and it is *not* invented. This distinction was
learned expensively: fourteen reports were once flagged for inventing a $595
price that is published verbatim on the page they cited.

So, in order:
- **Verified prices are the positive signal.** More independently confirmed
  figures is better grounding, full stop.
- A claim resting on a **citation that does not resolve** is a real defect.
- A **non-local fallback** — quoting a national market rate instead of finding a
  local price — is a real defect, and he reacted badly to exactly this.
- Unverified prices are neutral. Do not penalise them.

## OVERALL

Which report would he rather have received? **You must pick one.** Say whether
the preference is CLEAR or SLIGHT.

Weight the dimensions the way he did: local options and the four-week plan carry
the most, density matters more than you would expect from a generic rubric, the
decision separates least, grounding is a tiebreaker unless one report has a real
defect. But this is a judgment, not an average — if one report has a single
disqualifying failure, that can decide it on its own.

Give your reasoning as the single sentence you would say to him if he asked why.

# How to work

1. Read both reports the way he would: looking for his own dog, his own
   neighbourhood, and what he is supposed to do on Tuesday evening.
2. Do the deletable-passage pass on both BEFORE judging density. It is what stops
   the length reflex.
3. Judge the five dimensions. Quote both sides on every one.
4. Make the overall call. No ties.
5. Do not mention scores, points, or percentages anywhere. There is no scale here.

Return your answer through the structured output tool. No prose outside it.
